Must Know Quick Reference

Week 1 Content

Anatomy & Early Development

  • Umbilical cord: 2 arteries (carry waste away), 1 vein (carries oxygenated blood to the baby) — it's "backwards." On an exam, you want more A's.
  • Quickening (fetal movement felt by mom) by about 20 weeks.

Genetics

  • ★ You will have to build and interpret a Punnett square; the question will tell you if the disorder is dominant or recessive.
  • Know how traits are expressed: dominant needs 1 abnormal allele; recessive needs 2.

Signs, Dating & Assessment

  • ★ Naegele's Rule (you will calculate it): first day of LMP − 3 months + 7 days.
  • GTPAL — Gravida counts pregnancies, not babies (twins = one pregnancy), and includes the current one.
  • Fundal height should be within about 2 cm of gestational age in weeks.
  • Signs of pregnancy: presumptive (subjective) vs. probable (objective, e.g. a positive pregnancy test) vs. positive (only explained by pregnancy, e.g. the HCP palpates fetal movement).

Week 2 Content

Hypertensive Disorders

  • ★ Magnesium sulfate in preeclampsia is given to PREVENT SEIZURES — not to lower blood pressure.
  • Mag therapeutic level: 4 to 7.
  • Mag toxicity: absent reflexes, decreased respiratory rate, decreased level of consciousness / slurred speech. Antidote: IV calcium gluconate. (Expected side effects = flushing, warmth, drowsiness.)
  • Acute severe HTN meds — know drug, route, and contraindication (not doses): IV labetalol (avoid in asthma — "labetalol, lungs"); IV hydralazine (avoid in tachycardia); oral nifedipine (avoid in tachycardia).
  • Low-dose aspirin 81 mg for anyone at risk for preeclampsia (started 12 to 28 weeks).
  • Diagnosis of hypertension = TWO readings of 140/90 or higher, at least 4 hours apart.
  • Minimum urine output at least 30 mL/hr — be able to calculate it.

Diabetes & Cardiac Disorders

  • ★ Gestational diabetes glucose goals: fasting under 95, one hour under 140, two hours under 120.
  • 3-hour glucose tolerance test: 2 of 4 values elevated = gestational diabetes (don't memorize the exact cutoffs).
  • Cardiac decompensation — know the signs, not the individual disorders: new cough, dyspnea, edema, murmur, palpitations, rales, weight gain. Reduce cardiac workload with rest, an epidural in labor, and side-lying/semi-Fowler's positioning.

Fetal Testing & Screening

  • ★ CST is "backwards": Negative = reassuring (no late decelerations); Positive = bad (late decels with more than half of contractions).
  • Biophysical profile (BPP) components — BATMAN: Breathing, Amniotic fluid, Tone, Movement, plus the Non-Stress Test.
  • BPP scoring: each component is 0 or 2 (never a 1); if the score is not 8/8, do a non-stress test.
  • L/S ratio of 2:1 or higher = mature fetal lungs.
  • Quad screen / MSAFP: maternal blood at about 15 to 22 weeks; it is a SCREEN, not diagnostic.
  • AFP: HIGH = open neural tube defects (spina bifida, anencephaly), also multiples/diabetes; LOW = Down syndrome or trisomy 18.

Maternal Nutrition

  • ★ Folic acid: memorize at least 400 mcg/day (during pregnancy or when trying to conceive).

Week 3 Content

Labor & Birth (Intrapartum I)

  • ★ Fetal position from a picture — read the three-letter notation in order: side (R/L of the mother) · landmark (Occiput, Mentum, Sacrum, Acromion) · direction (Anterior, Posterior, Transverse). Find the landmark first. Occiput anterior (e.g., ROA) is the most favorable — the baby is born face-down.
  • Station: the ischial spines are zero station, the narrowest part of the pelvis; above = negative (−1 to −5), below = positive (+1 to +5). +4 = birth is imminent.
  • Dilation 0–10 cm; effacement 0–100%. Never push before 10 cm — pushing on an incomplete cervix can tear it or make it swell.
  • True labor = progressive cervical change. The stage/phase is judged by dilation (latent 0–3, active 4–7, transition 8–10); engagement = 0 station.
  • Contraction frequency = start of one contraction to the start of the next (in minutes).
  • Third stage ≤ 30 min (longer = hemorrhage / retained placenta risk); give oxytocin after the placenta delivers, then fundal massage.

Fetal Monitoring (Intrapartum II)

  • VEAL CHOP — decelerations by cause (and what to do): Variables = Cord compression (reposition first; think nuchal cord) · Early = Head compression (benign — do a vaginal exam, the baby's getting low) · Accelerations = Okay · Late = Placental insufficiency (a perfusion problem — always fix). Prolonged (≥2 min, <10) → prolapsed cord or abruption. Interventions = UNCOIL: change position (left side), Oxygen / turn Oxytocin off, IV fluid bolus (LR), Lower the head of the bed.
  • ★ Accelerations & NST reactive criteria (the gestational age will be given): ≥ 32 weeks = 15 bpm above baseline for 15 sec (reactive NST = 2 of them within 20 min); < 32 weeks = 10 bpm for 10 sec.
  • Variability is the BEST indicator of fetal oxygenation (moderate 6–25 is what you want; absent is ominous / category III).
  • Sinusoidal pattern = the baby may be acidotic → deliver, notify the provider immediately.
  • Montevideo units (IUPC): peak minus resting baseline, summed over 10 min — want >200 (≤300) for adequate labor; resting tone <25; tachysystole = ≥6 contractions in 10 min.
  • Best place to hear the FHR = over the fetal shoulders/back (use Leopold's to find it). For ROA, listen in the RLQ.
  • GBS: penicillin during labor (drug of choice); clindamycin 900 mg IV q4h if penicillin-allergic. Terbutaline for tachysystole — hold if maternal HR ≥120.
  • Triage tip: if she's thrashing and the toco isn't registering contractions, palpate the fundus first (rule out abruption/rupture) before readjusting the monitor or medicating.

Week 4 Content

Induction, Ripening & Oxytocin (Artificial Management of Labor)

  • Before an induction you need: a reactive NST, a sterile vaginal exam with a Bishop score, informed consent, and vital signs. (A non-reactive NST is a reason NOT to start.)
  • ★ Bishop score — know the 5 components, not the point values: cervical dilatation, effacement, position, consistency (4 cervical) + fetal station (1 fetal). The higher the score, the more likely a vaginal delivery; 8 or higher is favorable.
  • Amniotomy (AROM) uses: induce labor, augment labor, and allow internal monitoring (an FSE/IUPC needs ruptured membranes). First action after the water breaks or leaks = assess the FHR (lost cushion → cord compression).
  • Misoprostol (Cytotec): do NOT run it at the same time as oxytocin — space it, waiting at least 4 hours after the last dose.
  • Oxytocin: needs a reactive NST + Bishop score first; continuous fetal monitoring; titrate 1–2 milliunits/min every 30 min. Risks: tachysystole, uterine rupture, water intoxication / acute hyponatremia (stop the oxytocin, give NS + furosemide), and PPH after prolonged use. Never bolus it.
  • A fever with ruptured membranes → expect fetal tachycardia (>160 for ≥10 min): check mom's temperature FIRST to rule out infection.

Operative Birth, Cesarean & Anesthesia

  • Operative vaginal birth. Forceps: apply traction only while the client pushes; document when they are applied and removed, and assess for vaginal lacerations afterward. Vacuum: the baby must be low; expect scalp swelling and a jaundice risk from the bruising.
  • C-section — the skin incision does NOT have to match the uterine incision; document both. The uterine incision is what matters: low transverse is safest; a classical/vertical uterine incision means future births must be cesarean (rupture risk).
  • Cesarean prep: warm LR bolus (~1,500 mL), famotidine (Pepcid) + metoclopramide (Reglan) + Bicitra if unscheduled, Foley after anesthesia, and a wedge under the RIGHT hip (left tilt) to prevent supine hypotension — removed after birth. (Generic drug names on the exam.)
  • Contraindications to systemic analgesia: she is ready to push / imminent delivery (respiratory depression in the newborn) and a Category III tracing. Meperidine (Demerol) IV → expect decreased variability.
  • Spinal/epidural hypotension — prevent it with a pre-anesthetic fluid bolus. Treat it (systolic <100 + late decels) with an IV bolus, ephedrine 5–10 mg IV, oxygen, and turning her to the side.
  • General anesthesia is for emergent cases, a low platelet count, or when a spinal can't be placed; biggest risks are aspiration and PPH.

Preterm Labor, ROM & Cervical Insufficiency

  • Betamethasone for PPROM/preterm (under 34 weeks) → speeds fetal lung maturity; give 2 doses and try not to deliver within 48 hours of the first.
  • Tocolytics: nifedipine (hold if systolic <90), magnesium sulfate (relaxes smooth muscle; reflexes 2+ → 1+ is expected, absent is not), terbutaline SubQ (reassure her the racing/jittery feeling is a side effect), and progesterone.
  • Cervical insufficiency = painless dilation; treated with serial cervical-length ultrasounds, progesterone, and a cerclage. Discharge teaching after a cerclage: report uterine cramping or low back pain.

Bleeding, Fluid & Labor Complications

  • ★ Previa vs. abruption = abdominal pain. Previa is painless bleeding with a soft uterus (do NOT do a vaginal exam — confirm with ultrasound). Abruption is painful, board-like/rigid, and may be concealed. After a C-section for previa, the biggest risk is hemorrhage.
  • Normal AFI = 5–25 cm. Oligohydramnios risks: cord compression, growth/skeletal abnormalities, and pulmonary hypoplasia.
  • Amniotic fluid embolism: chest pain, dyspnea, frothy sputum, hypotension, cyanosis — support cardiorespiratory function and displace the uterus during CPR.
  • Dysfunctional labor is linked to an abnormal fetal presentation (e.g., transverse). Hypertonic = tachysystole; hypotonic = <2–3 weak contractions in 10 min.
  • Prolapsed cord — first action: keep gloved fingers in the vagina to lift the presenting part off the cord (then Trendelenburg, oxygen, notify, prep for cesarean).
  • Shoulder dystocia: McRoberts (flex hips, knees to chest) + suprapubic pressure; NEVER fundal pressure. Keeping a diabetic mom's glucose in range reduces macrosomia.
  • Retained placenta → manual removal, hemorrhage/infection risk. Abnormal adherence: accreta attaches, increta invades, percreta perforates.
  • Perinatal loss: ask the patient what she wants (where to recover, holding the baby, mementos); still give lactation-suppression teaching; in Kentucky, refer to KODA.

Week 5 Content

Breasts & Breastfeeding

  • Feed every 2–3 hours; NEVER more than 4 hours between newborn feedings, even a sleepy baby. 10–20 minutes per side, switch by 20 minutes. Alternate which breast she starts on — the baby sucks hardest at the start.
  • Mastitis vs. engorgement: mastitis is an infection — sudden onset, one breast, fever/chills/flu-like, a reddened painful area → antibiotics + analgesics, and keep breastfeeding or pumping. Engorgement is both breasts, hard and full, no fever.

Fundus, Lochia & Blood Loss

  • The fundal assessment: patient flat with an empty bladder, one hand supporting above the symphysis, the other pressing down at the fundus. Check that it is firm and midline and chart its level against the umbilicus (U/U, U−1…). Massage vigorously only if boggy or bleeding — massage irritates the uterus into clamping down and expresses clots (a clot-filled uterus can't contract). Deviated to one side = a full bladder; higher than yesterday = a complication.
  • Lochia: rubra (red, days 1–4) → serosa (pink, 4–10) → alba (white, day 10–20). A brownish phase in between is just older blood, and cesarean patients still have lochia. A foul/offensive odor means infection.
  • ★ Saturating a peripad in less than 1 hour is too much bleeding — no weighing needed. On a "who do you see first?" question, that patient is the priority.
  • ★ Quantitative blood loss is a likely math question. 1 gram = 1 mL. Weigh the used item, subtract the dry weight, and the difference in grams converts to mL. Excessive = >500 mL vaginal, >1,000 mL cesarean. Eyeballing (EBL) under-estimates.

Postpartum Hemorrhage

  • ★ The Four T's — what is causing the bleeding? Tone (atony) · Trauma (laceration) · Tissue (retained placenta) · Thrombin (coagulation disorder).
  • Uterine atony is the most significant risk factor for EARLY postpartum hemorrhage; retained placental fragments point to a LATE one. Risk factors for atony: a lot of oxytocin in labor (saturated receptors), multiparity, overdistension (multiples or a large baby), and magnesium sulfate.
  • ★ She is bleeding heavily — palpate the fundus FIRST. Boggy → fundal massage immediately. Firm but still free-flowing → suspect a laceration and call the provider (most tears are internal); meanwhile increase IV fluids and anticipate uterotonics.
  • Medications: oxytocin is first line (IV, or 10 units IM without access — never IV push). Methylergonovine (Methergine) is IM and contraindicated in hypertension — rebound hypertension. Carboprost (Hemabate) is the IM next line for a hypertensive patient — warn about explosive diarrhea. Misoprostol is given RECTALLY postpartum, because vaginal doses would wash out with the blood.

Perineum, Bladder & Bowel

  • To assess the perineum, place her side-lying and lift the upper leg, head of bed flat. Extreme perineal pain that persists after she was already medicated → assess the perineum — think hematoma.
  • An episiotomy is about a second-degree laceration. First-degree may or may not be sutured. Third and fourth degree get prophylactic antibiotics (they extend toward the rectum) plus stool softeners, fiber, and fluids.
  • ★ Anesthesia → bladder distension → urinary retention. She loses the sensation of filling, so the bladder still fills but nothing comes out.
  • ★ In a preeclamptic patient, INCREASING urine output is a good sign — those kidneys are very sensitive to poor perfusion, so more urine means perfusion is improving.
  • First bowel movement is around day 2–3. Use docusate sodium; avoid laxatives and enemas. Simethicone for gas — post-cesarean gas often refers to the right shoulder.

Emotions, Safety & Discharge

  • Taking in (days 1–2): passive and dependent, focused on herself — food and rest — and on processing the birth. Taking hold (days 2–3): takes control of mothering but needs reassurance she's doing it right.
  • Blues vs. depression. Baby blues: days 3–5, usually resolving in 10–14 days, more common in first-time moms; the hallmark is mood swings and crying for no apparent reason; self-limiting. Postpartum depression: onset around week 4, may appear up to 1 year out, the most under-diagnosed pregnancy-related complication — NOT mood swings, a sustained low mood, with suicide risk.
  • Strongest depression risk factors: a history of depression, a first pregnancy, and young maternal age (under 18, not over). The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale's last question asks about thoughts of self-harm — never skip it.
  • Postpartum psychosis: first 1–3 months; hyperagitation, insomnia, confusion, delusions and hallucinations, illogical thinking — risk of suicide and infanticide.
  • ★ A mother on magnesium sulfate must never be left alone with her baby — she is slower to arouse and respond. Another adult stays in the room, or the baby goes to the nursery.
  • Discharge criteria: she must void; a bowel movement is NOT required, but she must be passing flatus, and her episiotomy/laceration must be well approximated. Length of stay is about 48 hours vaginal, 3–4 days cesarean.
  • MMR if rubella non-immune or equivocal — it's a live virus, so postpartum (when we know she isn't pregnant) is the time to give it.
  • A new severe headache postpartum — which is it? Preeclampsia can appear for the first time AFTER delivery (readings like 150/100 and 160/90 on postpartum days 2–3). A spinal headache is positional — worse sitting up, better lying flat — with dizziness, photophobia, and a normal blood pressure.
