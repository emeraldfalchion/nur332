Postpartum Care & Complications

Physiologic Postpartum Changes

The postpartum period is the body's return toward its pre-pregnancy state. Always ask: is this finding a normal process of change, or a symptom of a complication?

System / ChangeWhat's Normal & What to Watch For
TemperatureMay be slightly elevated up to 100.4°F for the first 24 hours after birth, and again low-grade (~99°) when the milk comes in. Over 100.4°F — or any elevation after the first 24 hours — is a fever; look for a source of infection.
PulseBradycardia is normal during the first 6–10 days. Tachycardia can follow a difficult labor, or be a rebound reflex of hemorrhage — rule out bleeding, fever, respiratory disease, or pulmonary edema.
Blood pressureAn elevation should return to normal. High BP → preeclampsia, essential/chronic hypertension, renal disease, or anxiety. Low BP → suspect a uterine hemorrhage or hematoma. Preeclampsia can develop for the first time AFTER delivery — a new severe headache with readings such as 150/100 and 160/90 on the second and third postpartum days is postpartum preeclampsia.
Cardiac outputDeclines about 30% in the first two weeks; reaches normal by 6–12 weeks.
Postpartum diuresisThe kidneys must clear 2,000–3,000 mL of extra extracellular fluid — expect greatly increased urination and some weight loss. Failure to eliminate it can cause pulmonary edema (listen for crackles), especially in cardiac patients.
DiaphoresisProfuse sweating is normal, especially at night — part of clearing the extra fluid, along with the drop in pregnancy hormones. Reassure her; it is not a sign of infection by itself.
Shivering / "the shakes"Intense shivering in the first hour after birth, without feeling cold, is normal — the "labor shakes" or "epidural shakes," from the adrenaline still circulating. Warm blankets and reassurance.
RespirationsShould be non-labored and clear. Watch for fluid overload from IV boluses, oxytocin (acts like an antidiuretic hormone), and magnesium sulfate (can shift fluid to the lungs). Highest-risk patients: repeated IV boluses for epidural-related hypotension, prolonged oxytocin (water intoxication), and magnesium sulfate for preeclampsia (pulmonary edema).
HeadachesThe most common neurologic symptom, usually from a first-week fluid shift. Also consider a hypertensive disorder/preeclampsia (cerebral swelling), or stress and fatigue. Treat with acetaminophen ± caffeine; watch for visual spots or blurring. A spinal headache is recognized by being positional — it worsens every time she sits up and eases lying flat — with dizziness and photophobia and a normal blood pressure, which is what separates it from a hypertensive headache. Wet tap & blood patch covered again here — review in Artificial Management of Labor
Blood valuesA non-pathologic leukocytosis can occur in the first week — but a white count as high as 40,000 is NOT a normal postpartum change; look for infection. A 2–3% drop in hematocrit ≈ 500 mL blood loss. Plasma returns to pre-pregnancy levels by 4–6 weeks and platelets by about the 6th week; all values return to pre-pregnancy levels by the end of the 6-week period.
Weight lossAn initial 10–12 lb (baby, placenta, amniotic fluid) plus up to 5 lb of fluid in the diuresis. Many return near pre-pregnancy weight by 6–8 weeks — but there is no rush and she shouldn't feel pressured.
Return of menses / ovulationNot breastfeeding: menses in ~7–12 weeks; ovulation as early as 7–75 days. Breastfeeding: menses may be delayed 3+ months. Breastfeeding is NOT reliable birth control — ovulation can occur before the first period.
Nutrition: encourage iron-rich foods (blood loss) and continued prenatal vitamins, especially while breastfeeding. Breastfeeding moms increase intake by ~300 kcal/day; non-breastfeeding moms can decrease intake. Some need an iron supplement depending on blood loss.

Postpartum blood pressure medications (nifedipine XL, labetalol, methyldopa) and continued magnesium sulfate (usually left on at least 24 hours after delivery when started) may be used. Antihypertensives covered again here — review in Hypertensive Disorders

BUBBLE-HE: The Postpartum Assessment

BUBBLE-HE is the head-to-toe postpartum check:
B — Breasts · U — Uterus / abdomen · B — Bladder · B — Bowel · L — Lochia · E — Episiotomy, lacerations & incisions · H — Homans' sign & hemorrhoids · E — Emotions

Breasts & Lactation Suppression

Assess size, shape, and any reddened areas or engorgement (breasts become larger and full when the milk comes in). Check the nipples for cracks, fissures, soreness, or inversion — for a mom who wants to breastfeed, the nipple should be erect; flat or inverted nipples may need encouragement or a device to draw them out.

Suppressing Lactation (Mom Not Breastfeeding)

  • Wear a well-fitting bra, ace wrap, or binder to compress the breasts
  • Cold compresses or cabbage leaves; anti-inflammatories (ibuprofen) for tenderness
  • Avoid warm water / nipple stimulation — in the shower, keep her back to the warm water and just soap, rinse quickly. Any stimulation signals the body to make milk.

Breastfeeding

Supply and demand: the more the breast is emptied, the more milk the body makes. Feed every 2–3 hours (about 8–12 times/24 h), roughly 10–20 minutes starting on one breast, then switch by about 20 minutes — don't nurse longer than 20 minutes per side. The breast may feel heavy but should not be hard, sore, or reddened.

A newborn should never go longer than 4 hours between feedings — even a sleepy baby who "didn't act hungry." Waiting shortchanges the baby's nutrition and stops signaling the breast to keep producing.
Alternate which breast she starts on at each feeding. A hungry baby sucks hardest at the beginning of a feed, so always starting on the same side leaves the second breast less well emptied and the supply uneven.

Alcohol and medications: no alcohol for at least 2 hours before nursing. If she wants a drink, she can pump and dump that feeding's milk and give previously pumped milk instead — by the next feeding enough time has passed to nurse safely. She should clear every medication with her provider before breastfeeding; if a drug is contraindicated she pumps and dumps for the duration so the supply keeps up.

Positions & Latch

PositionNotes
CradleBaby's head rests on mom's arm, lying across her chest; good supported posture
Modified (cross) cradleOpposite arm from the feeding breast supports the head; same-side hand supports the breast
Football holdGreat for large breasts or after a C-section (nothing lies on the abdomen); the feeding-side hand supports the head, the other guides the breast
Side-lyingComfortable after abdominal surgery. The adult must not fall asleep — risk of rolling onto and smothering the baby (safe sleep: no co-sleeping)
A good latch: the mouth makes a fish shape with flared lips, taking in as much nipple AND areola as possible — not just the nipple. You should hear no smacking sounds. To fix a poor latch, put a finger in the baby's mouth to break the suction, take the baby off, and re-latch (stroking the chin opens the mouth wider).

Nipple shields (flexible) help with flattened nipples or a baby who isn't an aggressive feeder — the baby nurses through the holes; often no longer needed once feeding improves. Let the nipples air-dry after feeding, before replacing the bra or pads, to prevent breakdown.

Is the Baby Getting Enough?

Reassuring Signs

  • 6–8 wet diapers per day
  • Milk visible at the edges of the baby's mouth; audible swallowing during feeds
  • The breast feels softer after feeding (emptied) than before

For insufficient milk, make sure mom's own fluid intake is good — she may need to increase to ~2 liters of water a day (she must take in good fluid to make good fluid).

Common Concerns & Difficulties

Concerns Moms Raise

  • Nipple pain (usually a poor latch)
  • Embarrassment / privacy in public — offer cover-ups, private spaces
  • Feeling "tied down" — pumping lets someone else feed at times
  • Unequal feeding responsibility — involve the partner (diapering, giving expressed milk)
  • Worry the baby isn't getting enough — teach the reassuring signs above

Difficulties & Fixes

  • Sore nipples: good latch, lanolin cream, air-dry, rub in a little breast milk, start on the less sore side (baby sucks hardest first)
  • Flat nipples: roll with fingers to draw them out, or use a nipple shield
  • Plugged ducts: frequent nursing, change positions, manual massage + warm compresses; some start on the affected side; a pump may help — avoid pressure from purse straps, slings, seat belts

Weaning

Substitute one cup/bottle feeding for one breastfeeding session over several days, then gradually more over several weeks. A slow method is preferred — it prevents engorgement, lets the infant adjust its eating at its own rate, and gives time for psychological adjustment.

Uterus & Abdomen

After delivery the abdomen is loose and flabby but responds to exercise, and the uterine ligaments gradually return to their pre-pregnant state. Watch for diastasis recti abdominis — separation of the abdominal muscles that stretched apart to make room for the uterus, most likely after an over-distended abdomen (twins/triplets, a large baby). You can feel the gap by pressing your fingers into the midline at the umbilicus. It is usually NOT repaired: with time and exercise the muscles typically tighten back together, and surgical repair is reserved for the few it keeps bothering. Also note striae (stretch marks) — the color varies with the mother's own skin tone (pink, white, or silver) and they fade with time.

Afterpains

Afterpains are intermittent uterine contractions of involution (the uterus working back to its pre-pregnancy state). Comfort: rest prone with a pillow under the abdomen to keep the uterus contracted; ibuprofen/Motrin for cramping. Caution with Motrin — avoid if platelets are < 70,000 or she has a history of preeclampsia.

Involution & Fundal Assessment

Involution: after birth the fundus is at the level of the umbilicus by 6–12 hours, then descends about 1–2 cm (about 1 fingerbreadth) every 24 hours, reaching the true pelvis by about day 10. The uterus returns to pre-pregnancy size by ~5–6 weeks. The spongy decidua sloughs off (seen in the lochia); the placental site heals by exfoliation (a one-time bump in bright red blood as its "scab" sloughs is possible).

Documenting fundal position uses the umbilicus as the reference point. Always note that it is firm and midline, then where it sits: F / U−1 = firm, one fingerbreadth below the umbilicus; F / 1 U = firm, one fingerbreadth above; U / U = right at the level of the umbilicus, which is where it usually sits right after birth. To palpate: patient lying completely flat with an empty bladder (sitting up distorts the level), support the lower uterus above the symphysis with one hand and press down with the heel of the other, feeling for a firm "hardball."

Fingerbreadth measurement is somewhat subjective, so a one-fingerbreadth disagreement between nurses is expected. A large day-to-day discrepancy is not — and a fundus measuring higher than it did the day before is the worrying direction, suggesting the uterus is not contracting well and is filling with blood, clots, or retained placental fragments.

How much massage? If the fundus is already firm on your check, that is your check — don't rub it for five minutes. Vigorous massage is reserved for a boggy uterus or active bleeding. Unlike most muscles, massage irritates the uterus and makes it clamp down, which is exactly what you want postpartum. A firm downward massage also expresses clots — important, because a uterus full of clots cannot contract well.
A boggy (soft, spongy) fundus = uterine atony — the uterus is not clamping down, so bleeding increases. A fundus felt high or deviated to one side is usually pushed over by a full bladder, which also prevents it from contracting. Massage the fundus and have her empty her bladder.

Endometritis

A postpartum infection of the uterine lining. She complains of uterine tenderness, high fever, chills, and foul-smelling lochia.

Treatment: broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g., clindamycin + gentamicin) until culture/sensitivity results guide specific therapy; continue until she is afebrile for 24–48 hours.

Bladder & Bowel

Bladder

  • Postpartum (puerperal) diuresis is driven by mobilization of extracellular fluid — the body no longer needs the extra volume, so the kidneys clear 2,000–3,000 mL. She will void large amounts frequently; that is normal and means the kidneys are working
  • Increased bladder capacity from pregnancy; swelling and bruising around the urethra, plus small periurethral tears from birth — these are usually not sutured (stitches there would be more irritating than the tear) and heal on their own, though voiding may burn
  • Anesthesia → bladder distension → urinary retention. Losing the sensation of filling doesn't stop the bladder from filling — it just stops her feeling it, so urine collects and doesn't come out. Also a decreased sensitivity to fluid pressure
  • Increased infection risk (dilated ureters and renal pelvis)
  • Keep the bladder empty; monitor for distension; use an in-and-out cath if she is full and can't void (a full bladder deviates the uterus and increases bleeding). Measure output with a hat after a catheter comes out, and ask a vaginal-delivery patient whether she feels she is emptying fully
  • In a preeclamptic patient, rising urine output is a GOOD sign — preeclamptic kidneys are exquisitely sensitive to poor perfusion, so making more urine means perfusion is improving
  • UTI prevention: wipe front-to-back, peri-bottle rinse then pat dry, adequate fluids, void before/after intercourse, cotton underwear, cranberry to acidify urine

Bowel

  • The first bowel movement is usually around day 2–3 (some go during or right after delivery)
  • Often sluggish after birth — progesterone still slowing peristalsis, decreased muscle tone, pressure of the birth, and anesthesia (especially after C-section)
  • An episiotomy, laceration, or hemorrhoids can make her afraid to push for fear of tearing — but delaying only makes the stool harder and larger
  • Use docusate sodium (Colace) and other stool softeners. Avoid laxatives and enemas after a vaginal birth — you don't want a sudden, forceful, urgent movement against a fresh repair
  • Simethicone (Mylicon), a chewable gas tablet, for gas pain — common after a cesarean. Cesarean gas pain often refers to the right shoulder, so new right shoulder discomfort should make you think gas before anything exotic
  • Tucks pads for hemorrhoid itching and coolness, laid on top of the peripad; encourage regular bowel movements — constipation worsens perineal discomfort

Lochia

Lochia is the vaginal discharge of blood, mucus, and cells from the uterus. Describe it like a period: heavier and brighter at first, then lighter and darker over time. A change backward — e.g., pinkish then suddenly bright red again — should be reported.

StageTimingColor
Lochia rubraDays 1–4Red
Lochia serosaDays 4–10Pink
Lochia albaDay 10–20 (until the cervix closes)White / whitish

A brownish discharge is common as rubra transitions to serosa — it isn't a named category, it just means older blood rather than fresh. Cesarean patients still have lochia: even though the uterus was irrigated at surgery, the decidua still has to slough.

Amount is charted as scant, light, moderate, or heavy (saturating). Never should she saturate a pad in less than 1 hour — that suggests a postpartum hemorrhage.

Estimated vs. Quantitative Blood Loss

Describing the amount by eye — scant/light/moderate/heavy, or comparisons like "a quarter-sized" or "baseball-sized" stain — is estimated blood loss (EBL). It has value but is subjective, and under-estimating blood loss leads to poor maternal outcomes: a delivery charted at 350 mL plus several soft-uterus episodes upstairs may really have been 650 mL. Weighing gives quantitative blood loss (QBL), which catches a hemorrhage sooner. Counting can begin with the first item used at the birth, though most of the calculating happens once the delivery is finished.

Weighing blood loss (1 g ≈ 1 mL): weigh the saturated item and subtract the dry weight. Example: a soaked pad weighs 1,200 g and was 60 g dry → 1,200 − 60 = 1,140 g ≈ 1,140 mL of blood. This is a possible exam math calculation.
Excessive blood loss: more than 500 mL after a vaginal birth or more than 1,000 mL after a cesarean. Postpartum hemorrhage is a major cause of maternal morbidity and mortality both in this country and worldwide.

Postpartum Hemorrhage

The Four T's — what is causing the bleeding?
Tone — uterine atony · Trauma — a laceration · Tissue — retained placenta · Thrombin — a coagulation disorder

Early vs. late matters. Uterine atony is the single most significant risk factor for EARLY postpartum hemorrhage (soon after birth). Retained placental fragments are more a risk factor for a LATE hemorrhage.

Signs of postpartum hemorrhage: excessive or bright red bleeding (saturating > 1 pad/hour), a boggy fundus that doesn't respond to massage, abnormal clots, a rising fundal level, persistent bleeding despite a firmly contracted uterus, high temperature, unusual pelvic discomfort/backache, bulging shiny perineal skin (hematoma), increased pulse / decreased BP, and decreased level of consciousness.

Prevention & Nursing Care

She calls out that she is bleeding heavily — what do you do FIRST? Palpate the fundus. Go to the place the problem is most likely coming from. Vital signs would only describe what you already see; assessing the fundus tells you why it is happening so you can fix it.
  • Boggy → start fundal massage immediately, then uterotonics.
  • Firm and midline but still free-flowing (or squirting each time you press) → suspect a laceration and call the provider to examine her — most tears are internal and can't be seen from outside. While you wait: apply pressure to any visible external tear, increase her IV fluids, and anticipate medications to control the bleeding.

What the Nurse Does

  • Fundal massage — do not neglect or delay it; if the uterus feels soft, massage immediately
  • Assess the perineum for free-flow bleeding or clots at the same time as the fundal check
  • Encourage frequent voiding; catheterize if the bladder is full and she can't empty it
  • Watch hematocrit; repeat a CBC on the first postpartum day; encourage iron-rich foods ± supplement
  • Teach her to rise slowly to minimize orthostatic hypotension if her count is low

MedicationKey Points

Perineum, Episiotomy & Incision Care

Note cervical/vaginal changes; the cervix is often spongy, flabby, and bruised, its external os becoming a lateral slit (a diaphragm must be refitted). The vagina is edematous with small superficial lacerations; rugae reappear in ~3–4 weeks and it returns to pre-pregnancy state by ~6 weeks. Note the type of episiotomy (midline vs. mediolateral) and the degree of any laceration, and confirm it is approximated and not bleeding.

Perineal & Hemorrhoid Care

To assess the perineum, put her in a side-lying (Sims') position and lift the upper leg, with the head of the bed flat. Sitting her up works against you — gravity settles her onto her bottom and hides the area. Knee-chest is not a position we use postpartum, and mother-baby beds have no stirrups for lithotomy.
Extreme perineal pain that persists after she has already been medicated → go look. Assess the perineum first — unrelieved pain out of proportion to the repair points to something the analgesic can't fix, such as a hematoma.
REEDA assesses a repaired perineum/laceration: Redness, Edema, Ecchymosis (bruising), Discharge, and Approximation (how well it lines up).

Cesarean Incision Care

Nursing Care After a C-Section

  • Assess the incision using REEDA; it may be closed with staples (removed with a staple remover, often followed by Steri-Strips) or with Dermabond skin glue. In the shower, let soapy water run over it, then pat dry; leave Steri-Strips to peel off on their own
  • Minimize respiratory complications: deep breathing, incentive spirometer, early ambulation
  • Encourage rest (hard with a newborn) and pain management — avoid narcotics if possible, using ibuprofen/acetaminophen and reserving narcotics for when needed
  • Minimize gas pains: simethicone (chewable gas tablet) and ambulation

Homans' Sign & Thrombophlebitis

Homans' sign screens for thrombophlebitis. Technique: support behind the knee with one hand and cradle the sole with the other, let the weight of the leg rest in your hand and the foot go loose (moving it around a little helps her relax it), then quickly dorsiflex — the same motion used to check for clonus. Calf pain is a positive sign. Some units avoid it entirely, preferring not to disturb a clot that may be there, and rely on comparing the legs instead — follow your unit's policy and provider orders.

What to compare between legs: calf size (is one bigger?), redness, warmth or any temperature change, swelling, and tenderness to touch. A clot is typically one-sided. Pain may not be limited to the calf — ask about the leg, the groin, and even the lower abdomen, and about pain when she walks.

Preventing Thrombophlebitis

  • Avoid prolonged standing/sitting and crossing the legs; take breaks to walk on long car rides
  • SCDs (sequential compression devices) on the lower legs when she can't ambulate (e.g., after a C-section)
  • Early ambulation — promotes circulation and reduces constipation, respiratory dysfunction, and clots, and improves overall well-being

Postpartum Mood Disorders

ConditionFrequency & TimingFeatures
Postpartum blues 50–80% of mothers; days 3–5, up to 6 weeks; resolves naturally in ~10–14 days Transient, self-limiting. Mood swings — sadness interspersed with happy feelings, irritability, oversensitivity, episodic cheerfulness, trouble sleeping, feeling let down, anxiety. Driven by rapid drops in estrogen, progesterone, and prolactin, plus fatigue and an emotional letdown after birth
Postpartum depression 3–30%; greatest onset around the 4th week; may appear up to 1 year after birth The most under-diagnosed pregnancy-related complication. Not mood swings — a persistent down feeling. May contemplate suicide as escape or to "protect" the baby; dying may seem preferable. Disrupts bonding, lactation, and newborn development; untreated can become major depression
Postpartum psychosis 1–2 per 1,000 births; first 1–3 months; 90–95% improve with treatment Agitation, hyperactivity, insomnia, confusion, poor memory/concentration; delusions or hallucinations, very illogical thinking. Risk of suicide and infanticide

Postpartum blues are more common in first-time mothers — it is a very big life event she has not been through before.

Depression risk factors: the strongest are a history of depression (general, bipolar, or a previous postpartum depression), a first pregnancy, and young maternal age (under 18). Also: ambivalence toward the pregnancy, poor social support, dissatisfaction with self/body image, history of abuse, and socioeconomic hardship. Breastfeeding and chronic illness such as diabetes are not specific risk factors for postpartum depression, though either can weigh on mood.

The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale is the screening tool. Its last question asks directly about thoughts of harming yourself — never skip it. It is tempting to assume a new mother with a healthy baby couldn't feel that way; don't make assumptions about what someone is struggling with internally. Patients answer honestly far more often than you would expect when they feel listened to and not judged.

Supporting a Mother With the Blues

  • Anticipatory guidance — tell her before discharge that she may feel this way and that most mothers do, and that it settles as her confidence grows
  • Validate what she is feeling rather than talking her out of it
  • Protect rest and nutrition — ask family and friends to bring easy, ready-to-eat food and to take the baby so she can sleep
  • Rooming-in so she learns infant cues and builds confidence before going home
  • Include the support person in all of the teaching, including what would signal this is no longer the blues
Depression and psychosis require a referral to a mental health professional — not something she manages alone. Provide anticipatory guidance before discharge so she and her partner know the symptoms to watch for (people often don't recognize it in themselves). First-line treatment: cognitive behavioral therapy; if medication is needed, sertraline (Zoloft) is first-line, then paroxetine (Paxil) — avoid Prozac while breastfeeding. Psychosis may need lithium, antipsychotics, or electroconvulsive therapy, and the baby may need to be removed from the home for safety.

Bonding, Discharge & Teaching

Attachment & Bonding

Bonding is a gradual exploration — fingertips, then palms, then enfolding and holding. Moms often use the en face position (direct, face-to-face eye contact). The partner's version is called engrossment: absorbed watching and interest, often at a little more distance, and asking "does everything look okay?" while you assess the baby. Promote bonding with rooming-in, kangaroo care (skin-to-skin), private acquaintance time, telling the birth story, and involving siblings. Individualize cultural and religious care by simply asking the patient what matters to her.

The golden hour — the first hour after birth. Keep the room quiet and the visitor list short: skin-to-skin, the first breastfeeding, and time for the family to take in the birth. Parents may ask to delay eye prophylaxis (erythromycin ointment) up to an hour, since the ointment makes the baby reluctant to open its eyes.

Also help them prepare for adjustment problems — a known birth defect or a NICU stay, a first-time parent role, or an added sibling changing the family dynamic — and help them identify, understand, and accept their own feelings. Not every parent arrives excited; some are only now coming to accept an unplanned pregnancy. Minimize fatigue: rest is part of healing.

Safety — a mother on magnesium sulfate must never be left alone with her baby. Magnesium slows her arousal and response, so another responsible adult has to be in the room. If no support person is available, the baby goes to the nursery until the magnesium is finished. She may absolutely keep the baby with her — she just cannot be the only adult there.

Activity, Sexual Activity & Rest

Home Teaching

  • Rest when the baby sleeps; accept outside help (let visitors hold the baby while she naps)
  • Avoid heavy lifting, frequent stair climbing, and strenuous activity while the uterus and pelvic muscles recover
  • Sexual activity: the rule is not really a date — it is that everything must be healed and the lochia stopped. People say "6 weeks" because that is when the postpartum visit happens and the provider can confirm healing. Resuming over an unhealed laceration or episiotomy risks friction, bleeding, loosened sutures, and infection. Lubrication may be needed for vaginal dryness
  • Contraception — the conversation has to happen. Ask what she plans to use (abstinence, condoms, an implant, a depot shot, oral contraceptives). Women have come back pregnant at the 6-week visit, and that short an interval doesn't give the body time to recover. Fatigue and the demands of the infant may lower desire

Transfer & Discharge Criteria

Transferring to the postpartum unit: the nurse (not the provider) prepares the report, and giving it as a bedside report improves client safety and satisfaction. After general anesthesia, the anesthesiologist decides when she is ready to move. Apgar scoring belongs to the birth — it is done at 1 and 5 minutes of life, not on arrival to the unit.

Typical length of stay: about 48 hours after a vaginal birth; 3–4 days after a cesarean (most go home on day 3), absent complications.

Ready for Discharge When…

  • Stable vital signs — no fever, acceptable blood pressure and heart rate
  • Uterus firm, midline, and involuting; appropriate lochia with no signs of infection (and she knows the signs — especially foul-smelling discharge or drainage from an incision)
  • Episiotomy/laceration well approximated — not healed yet, but lined up so it can heal; she can do peri care and take medications as ordered
  • She must void. A bowel movement is NOT required — many go home before day 2–3 — but she does need to be passing flatus, showing the bowels have woken up, and be able to eat and drink without difficulty
  • She knows the symptoms of postpartum depression and resources; demonstrates self- and baby-care and appropriate interaction; reviewed infant safety
  • Appropriate interaction with the baby. She doesn't have to hold the infant every moment, but never seeing her interact is a red flag worth investigating — while remembering that in some cultures the family does most of the baby care so the mother can rest. Ask whether it is normal for this patient
  • Vaccines if needed — give MMR if she is rubella non-immune or equivocal. It is a live virus, so it can't be given during pregnancy; postpartum, when you are confident she is not pregnant, is the right time. If she is Rh-negative with an Rh-positive baby and a negative direct Coombs, she receives RhoGAM. RhoGAM & direct Coombs covered again here — review in Antepartum Complications
  • Aware of resources: home health, follow-up calls, breastfeeding/infant CPR classes, support groups

What does BUBBLE-HE stand for?
Breasts, Uterus, Bladder, Bowel, Lochia, Episiotomy/lacerations/incisions, Homans' sign & hemorrhoids, Emotions.
What are the Four T's of postpartum hemorrhage?
Tone (uterine atony), Trauma (a laceration), Tissue (retained placenta), Thrombin (a coagulation disorder).
How fast does the fundus normally descend during involution?
About 1–2 cm (roughly one fingerbreadth) every 24 hours, reaching the true pelvis by about day 10.
What does a boggy fundus indicate, and what causes it to deviate to one side?
A boggy fundus = uterine atony. A fundus high or deviated to one side is usually pushed over by a full bladder.
Name the three stages of lochia with their colors.
Rubra (red, days 1–4), serosa (pink, days 4–10), alba (white, day 10–20).
A client 1 hour postpartum calls out that she is bleeding heavily. What is your FIRST action?
Palpate the fundus — go to the most likely source. (Saturating a pad in under 1 hour is too much bleeding.) If it is boggy, massage immediately; if it is firm and she is still bleeding, suspect a laceration and call the provider.
The fundus is firm but she keeps bleeding bright red. What do you suspect?
A laceration of the genital tract — the provider needs to check.
Which postpartum hemorrhage medication must be avoided in a patient with high blood pressure?
Methylergonovine (Methergine) — it can cause rebound hypertension. Use carboprost (Hemabate) instead.
Mastitis vs. engorgement: which is usually one-sided with a fever?
Mastitis (infection) — sudden, one side, with a fever. Engorgement is gradual, often both breasts, without a high fever.
How do you calculate quantitative blood loss, and what counts as excessive?
1 gram = 1 mL. Weigh the used item, subtract its dry weight, and convert the difference in grams to mL. Excessive is more than 500 mL after a vaginal birth or more than 1,000 mL after a cesarean.
Postpartum blues vs. postpartum depression: which involves mood swings, and which is a persistent down feeling?
Blues = mood swings, self-limiting in 10–14 days. Depression = persistent down feeling, peaks ~week 4, may need referral and sertraline.
Compare the "taking in" and "taking hold" maternal phases.
Taking in (days 1–2): passive, dependent, focused on herself and the birth. Taking hold (day 2–3): resumes control of mothering, more autonomous, needs reassurance.
